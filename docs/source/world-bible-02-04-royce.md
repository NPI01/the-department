THE DEPARTMENT
World Bible 02 — Royce Industries, 1977, and the High Plains Conspiracy

October 1977. Somewhere on the northern Great Plains.
You've just accepted a position with Royce Industries, a privately owned research and development company whose headquarters occupy several hundred acres of isolated prairie. The compensation is excellent, the employee benefits are unusually generous, and your relocation expenses have been paid in full.
Nobody seems particularly interested in telling you what the company actually manufactures.
Royce employs engineers, physicists, biologists, accountants, computer programmers, security personnel, administrative specialists, and research analysts. Different divisions work on different problems. Projects are compartmentalized. Employees seldom know what colleagues in adjoining buildings are developing.
Your employment agreement contains an extraordinary confidentiality clause.
On your third day, you receive a memorandum instructing you to attend a meeting in a building that does not appear on the facility map.
On your fifth day, a local rancher calls your office demanding an explanation for several dead cattle discovered along Royce property.
On your seventh day, a colleague asks whether you've seen the black helicopters.
And sometime during your second week, someone slides an envelope beneath your office door.
Inside is a photograph of a ritual gathering.
On the back, someone has written:
They're not worshipping it. They're trying to keep it asleep.
Welcome to The Department.

1. First, the geography and historical setting
I would anchor this much more firmly in actual 1970s history than our original concept.
One small geographical correction: Minot is in North Dakota, not South Dakota. I'd place Royce Industries at a fictional campus in north-central North Dakota, perhaps 40–60 miles from Minot. South Dakota can still become important through a separate installation in the Black Hills.
This gives us access to a remarkable real historical landscape.
By 1977, Minot Air Force Base was already an important Strategic Air Command installation. Minuteman III missiles had been operational there since 1970, and the surrounding countryside contained a vast dispersed missile complex.
There's also a crucial historical UFO event: on October 24, 1968, military personnel near Minot reported unusual aerial objects, and a B-52 crew reported radar and visual observations. The interpretation remains disputed, but the records provide excellent material for a fictional corporate investigation originating in 1968.
I would make the real history the substrate of the game, then invent a fictional layer beneath it.
The cattle mutilations, secret helicopter sightings, UFO reports, nuclear anxieties, apocalyptic religious movements, and institutional paranoia should all seem to converge on Royce Industries.
But we shouldn't initially explain why.
And I would preserve a vital distinction: historical people and institutions provide background, contemporaneous news, philosophical influences, and period authenticity. The invented criminal conspiracy belongs to our fictional characters and organizations, not to real people for whom such involvement has no historical evidentiary basis.

2. Royce Industries: The company that shouldn't exist
Royce Industries was incorporated in Delaware in 1959 by industrialist Edmund H. Royce, a former electronics entrepreneur who made his fortune through military instrumentation and precision manufacturing.
Initially, Royce produced avionics components, specialized sensors, industrial control systems, and laboratory instruments.
Its rapid expansion began in 1969.
That year, the company acquired approximately 600 acres in North Dakota and announced construction of a major research facility dedicated to high-energy physics, advanced materials, electromagnetic systems, and aerospace instrumentation.
Its corporate literature promised a technological future founded upon American ingenuity.
The company opened the campus in 1972.
By 1977, Royce is among the region's largest private employers, though its actual revenue and ownership structure are difficult to determine.
Why the company is interesting
Royce shouldn't appear sinister on the surface.
It has a modern cafeteria, a recreational association, company baseball teams, respectable salaries, and a remarkably generous pension system. Employees receive holiday newsletters. The company sponsors local science fairs.
But the institution has peculiar characteristics.
Some research divisions appear to produce no marketable products. Certain projects receive equipment far beyond their publicly stated budgets. Restricted buildings have their own power supplies. A nighttime flight operation has no corresponding aviation department.
And one laboratory is regularly supplied with biological material even though the company claims to have discontinued its animal research program.
The players aren't hired to investigate this.
They're hired to work there.
The mystery emerges because ordinary employees gradually discover that the company they work for makes no sense.

3. The Department is a real division within Royce
I would preserve The Department as our game's title, but redefine its meaning.
Within Royce Industries, there's an internal administrative unit formally designated:
Department of Special Research and Development — DSRD
Among employees, it's simply called The Department.
The DSRD coordinates sensitive company projects, handles unusual technical requirements, resolves interdepartmental disputes, and manages projects requiring specialized clearance.
Players are newly hired or newly transferred employees working throughout Royce's departments, all of whom find themselves drawn into DSRD-related matters.
They need not begin with full knowledge of DSRD, and they certainly shouldn't all know about the company's defense contracts.
I'd give the company eight principal departments.
Department | What the employee thinks they do | What they might eventually discover
Advanced Materials | Metallurgy and exotic alloys | Materials with undocumented origins
Aerospace Systems | Aviation and guidance | Unconventional propulsion experiments
Biological Sciences | Veterinary and medical research | Biological sampling linked to mutilation cases
Applied Physics | Energy systems and instrumentation | Devices that produce anomalous effects
Information Systems | Computing and statistical analysis | Research involving consciousness, signals, and prediction
Procurement & Logistics | Purchasing and transportation | Unexplained equipment, shipments, and financial relationships
Corporate Security | Industrial security and investigations | Covert field operations and outside intelligence contacts
Personnel & Administration | Employment and internal policy | Compartmentalized identities and irregular hiring practices
This is ideal for our game engine because each department can have its own authority, resources, restricted information, and capabilities.
A procurement officer might discover something a physicist cannot. A personnel analyst might discover a mysterious employee whose records contradict company history. A security employee might be asked to suppress a report that a biological researcher has independently confirmed.
No one initially sees the complete picture.

Part II — The Conspiracy Beneath Royce Industries
4. What is Royce actually developing?
I would create several secret research programs that appear unrelated, even to the scientists working on them.
The story begins as industrial espionage and strange science, then gradually expands into occultism, UFOs, nuclear strategy, and cosmic horror.
PROJECT ORPHEUS — Advanced Materials. An experimental material that responds unpredictably to electromagnetic fields. Its internal structure cannot be reproduced by known manufacturing techniques. Laboratory personnel disagree over whether it was discovered, synthesized, or recovered.
PROJECT CHOIR — Information Systems. Research into synchronized human cognition, statistical anomalies, and signal recognition. Tests suggest that certain people can detect information before it is conventionally available. Some subjects report hearing the same voice.
PROJECT NIGHTJAR — Aerospace Systems. Experimental aerial vehicles based on a propulsion phenomenon that does not fit conventional engineering theory. Test activity has become increasingly difficult to conceal. Some reported UFOs may be Royce prototypes.
PROJECT HARVEST — Biological Sciences. A classified study of livestock tissue, environmental contamination, and unusual biological responses. Researchers have discovered repeatable changes in samples gathered from particular regions of the High Plains.
PROJECT JANUS — Applied Physics. A highly restricted program studying how extreme energy releases affect anomalous materials. Its connection to nuclear research is classified, and even its senior investigators disagree about the purpose of the experiments.
The important thing is that these programs should produce real game mechanics, not merely clues.
An employee who gains access to ORPHEUS could acquire valuable materials. Someone in CHOIR could develop information-gathering capabilities at considerable personal risk. NIGHTJAR might provide experimental surveillance assets. HARVEST could yield scientific discoveries. JANUS could become a major source of season-ending danger.

5. Where is the technology coming from?
This should be one of the game's central mysteries, and I'd deliberately construct several competing explanations.
Four competing origin theories. These are beliefs that circulate within the fictional world, not established facts revealed to the players.
Theory A — Recovered extraterrestrial technology. Royce received recovered materials following an aerial incident. The military established the company as a private research cutout to keep the technology outside ordinary government oversight.
Theory B — An indigenous terrestrial phenomenon. The technology is derived from something found deep beneath the northern plains. UFO activity may actually be a side effect of its interaction with Earth's environment.
Theory C — Human technology from an impossible future. Some objects contain engineering features and information that appear to originate from a future technological civilization. Royce isn't reverse-engineering aliens; it may be reverse-engineering humanity's future.
Theory D — The technology is an interface. The devices are not independent inventions. They are mechanisms through which an external intelligence can interact with the physical world. Operating them may be a form of communication—or invitation.
My preference is that none of these theories completely explains the situation.
The best mystery would be one in which players can obtain authentic evidence supporting seemingly incompatible explanations.
A recovered object may genuinely have an unknown metallurgical structure. Another object may have been manufactured by Royce. A third may contain information that no one involved could reasonably have known.
Rather than designing a simple reveal that "the aliens did it," I'd make the players confront a much stranger question:
Is Royce manufacturing weapons from anomalous technology, or has the technology been encouraging Royce to manufacture them?
That creates the possibility that the institution itself is participating in a process it doesn't understand.

6. The Devil Cult
I would make the Devil Cult the game's second great mystery, with a very different logic from the company's scientific research.
Local newspapers refer to a so-called Devil Cult following reports of animal mutilations, vandalism, strange symbols, and nighttime gatherings.
But that label conceals several competing possibilities.
Some members of the organization describe themselves as practitioners of a much older religious tradition. Others regard their activities as entirely secular. Some believe they are protecting the world from an ancient intelligence. Others believe that intelligence represents the next stage of evolution.
Even their name should be uncertain.
The press calls them the Devil Cult.
Internally, one faction calls itself The Vigil.
Another uses the name The Congregation of the Last Light.
And the oldest documents refer only to the Keepers.
The cult's central doctrine
The original members believe that something exists beneath the High Plains.
It is not a demon in the ordinary Christian sense. Nor is it necessarily an extraterrestrial being.
It is described in fragmentary documents as a consciousness that has existed alongside humanity for an immense period of time.
Its influence appears through dreams, electrical phenomena, unusual lights, altered perceptions, and recurrent patterns in human religious experience.
Some members believe the entity is imprisoned. Others believe it is asleep.
The distinction is critically important.
Those who believe it is imprisoned want to release it. Those who believe it is asleep want to prevent its awakening. Those who believe it is already awake want to determine what it is doing.
This opens up the possibility that several apparently opposing groups are all being described by outsiders as the same Devil Cult.
The cult and Royce
I would establish a complicated relationship rather than decide that the cult controls the corporation.
Certain Royce executives may belong to the Vigil. Other executives may be trying to infiltrate it. One of the company's original investors might have funded its research after receiving strange information from an individual associated with the organization.
At the same time, some cult members may be trying to sabotage Royce because they believe its experiments threaten the world.
This allows a player to discover evidence of a satanic ritual and mistakenly conclude that the cult is conducting malevolent operations, when the ritual might actually be intended to interfere with a dangerous experiment.
But we should also permit genuinely dangerous cult factions.
One faction could regard the ancient intelligence as something that must be awakened regardless of the consequences. Another could believe that the world's destruction is the necessary precondition for its rebirth.

7. Players can secretly join the Devil Cult
This is an excellent mechanic, particularly if the player receives the offer after they've already developed relationships within Royce.
I'd avoid making cult membership a standard starting choice for everyone.
Instead, select players receive invitations based on their roles, background traits, early discoveries, and the needs of the simulation.
Some invitations are genuine. Others might come from informants, infiltrators, or rival sects.
A player can accept, refuse, report the invitation to Corporate Security, or attempt to infiltrate the organization while concealing their actual loyalties.
SAMPLE PRIVATE CORRESPONDENCE · ROUND 05 — An Invitation
You have been told that the company is engaged in research that will advance the human condition. You have been lied to. There are things beneath the prairie that Royce did not discover and cannot control. We have been watching your work. If you want to know what really happened at the north installation, come to the old pumping station after sunset. Do not bring your employee identification. Do not tell Corporate Security. You will be offered a choice. — The Vigil
(Options: Attend the meeting discreetly / Report the message to Corporate Security / Attempt to recruit an ally and infiltrate the meeting / Ignore the invitation)
The cult could have its own communications network, internal hierarchy, resource economy, voting procedures, and secret objectives.
Crucially, cult membership shouldn't necessarily mean betrayal of Royce. A player might genuinely believe that protecting the company requires participation in the Vigil.
And some of the most interesting players might be double agents who discover that neither organization is telling them the complete truth.

Part III — Weaving the Real 1970s Into the Fiction
8. Cattle mutilations and the black helicopters
This is one of the strongest historical elements we can incorporate because it was already a major topic in newspapers during the exact period of our game.
In October 1975, both The Washington Post and The New York Times reported widespread cattle mutilation concerns in the western United States. Explanations ranged from predators and scavengers to human intervention, alleged cult activity, and unidentified helicopters. These reports establish the atmosphere of public fear, not proof of a coordinated occult or military operation.
In The Department, I would make the mutilations the place where multiple plotlines intersect.
Local ranchers suspect Royce Industries. Royce's biological division denies involvement, but company logistics records indicate unexplained rural operations. Corporate Security reports unidentified helicopters operating near the test grounds.
The Devil Cult claims the company is collecting something from the animals. An Air Force contact suspects a secret civilian weapons program. And a researcher finds evidence that some animals were subjected to unusual electromagnetic conditions before death.
The black helicopters should not all be the same thing
I would create at least three distinct sources of helicopter activity within the fiction.
Some aircraft are genuine Royce contractors conducting classified operations. Others belong to federal or military organizations working independently. Still others are unidentified and may not be conventional helicopters at all.
That creates opportunities for misidentification.
A player might spend several rounds investigating a particular helicopter flight, only to discover that it has nothing to do with the cattle mutilations. Another might identify a real Royce aircraft but reach the wrong conclusion about its mission.
By making these events partially independent, we create a world that is more convincing than a single conspiracy in which every mysterious event has the same cause.

9. The nuclear question: Is someone trying to acquire a bomb?
This is where the stakes escalate from local mystery to Cold War catastrophe.
Royce's proximity to Minot's nuclear facilities gives the game a constant atmosphere of strategic danger.
The fictional Devil Cult might believe that a nuclear detonation at a particular location would awaken—or destroy—the intelligence beneath the Plains. A rival faction could interpret the same prophecy differently, concluding that an extreme energy release is required to seal a breach or prevent a larger disaster. Meanwhile, some Royce scientists may be studying the interaction between anomalous materials and high-energy physical events without subscribing to any supernatural interpretation.
The questions for players become:
- Is somebody genuinely trying to obtain a nuclear weapon, or has a scientific proposal been misinterpreted?
- Is the alleged threat originating inside Royce, the cult, or a third organization?
- Is an explosive device the actual objective, or is access to nuclear research and testing the more important prize?
- Can the players prevent a catastrophe without knowing whether the supposed threat is even real?
For gameplay, I'd treat nuclear assets as tightly controlled scenario-level entities, not ordinary items that players can obtain by accumulating enough resources. The drama can center on authorization, intelligence, interdiction, and conflicting evidence without simulating actionable procedures for acquiring or operating an actual nuclear weapon.

10. UFOs: The 1968 Minot incident becomes the founding mystery
In our fictional history, Royce Industries experiences its first extraordinary expansion immediately after 1968.
The historical October 24, 1968 Minot UFO reports provide a perfect chronological anchor.
What if the company's most restricted technologies appeared in its research records shortly afterward?
A player studying corporate finances might discover that Royce abruptly abandoned several conventional programs during 1969 and received a massive influx of private investment. An engineer might discover that an early prototype bears a manufacturing date of November 1968 despite using techniques far beyond Royce's documented capabilities. An archivist might find a missing technical annex referring to an event called RECOVERY NORTH. And a security officer might learn that several early Royce personnel previously served at Minot Air Force Base.
All of those would be fictional additions to a genuine historical backdrop.
The 1968 incident becomes the first major question: What happened on the night of October 24, 1968, and why did Royce Industries change afterward?

11. The United Nations, Maurice Strong, and Crestone
Maurice Strong led the 1972 Stockholm environmental conference and served as the founding executive director of the United Nations Environment Programme until 1975. His involvement in developing Crestone into a spiritual center belongs chiefly to the period beginning around 1978, just after our 1977 setting.
In 1977, Crestone is not yet the well-established spiritual center it later becomes. Our fictional world can present obscure land transactions, exploratory visits, correspondence, and interest in the San Luis Valley as an emerging mystery whose historical significance players do not yet understand.
But I would not depict Strong personally as secretly organizing a cult or directing a UN invasion as an established historical fact. Those would be fictional conspiracy claims, most effectively carried by invented intermediaries and organizations.
The fictional international program
I'd invent an organization called the International Geophysical Coordination Commission (IGCC).
It is a multinational scientific and environmental consortium that receives cooperation or attention from UN-affiliated institutions, universities, and private foundations.
Publicly, it studies environmental monitoring, geophysical anomalies, and the ecological consequences of advanced industrial technology. Privately, some of its researchers have identified a pattern linking anomalous events across several locations around the world.
Royce considers the IGCC a threat because it could expose classified American technology. The IGCC believes Royce may be suppressing information relevant to a global environmental or technological danger. And a faction inside the Department becomes convinced that IGCC personnel are preparing for something more sinister.
The UN invasion rumor
I would make the feared UN invasion a rumor that develops into a gameplay threat.
Players encounter documents discussing multinational emergency intervention, environmental containment, and international observation. A paranoid Corporate Security analyst interprets the material as preparation for foreign troops occupying American territory. A faction deliberately leaks misleading documents to local political groups.
The result is an increasingly volatile atmosphere of militia organizing, suspicion, and potential violence.
Whether an actual foreign operation exists is something players can investigate.

12. Teilhard de Chardin and the Omega Point
Pierre Teilhard de Chardin proposed an evolutionary vision in which life develops increasing complexity and consciousness, tending toward a culmination he called the Omega Point. His account involves the noosphere—the sphere of thought and collective consciousness—and a fundamentally spiritual understanding of evolutionary convergence.
I would make this philosophy central to several Royce research programs, while allowing different factions to radically reinterpret it.
The Omega Hypothesis
A senior Royce scientist develops a controversial theory: Consciousness is not merely an outcome of biological complexity. Under certain conditions, networks of consciousness may interact with a physical process that ordinary scientific instruments cannot detect.
The researcher suspects that the anomalous technologies are exploiting that process.
Some devices appear to function only when groups of people are exposed to synchronized stimuli. Others produce different outcomes depending on the expectations of their operators.
The scientist begins referring to the research as OMEGA.
The Instrumentalists believe they have discovered a new source of technological power. The Vigil believes Royce is attempting to artificially accelerate a process that should never be forced. A radical cult faction believes the intelligence beneath the Plains is itself the Omega Point. Another faction argues that the entity is the exact opposite: an ancient force that prevents humanity from reaching its evolutionary culmination.
Are the technologies moving humanity toward its destiny, or interrupting that destiny? And are the people who oppose the experiments enlightened protectors—or terrified reactionaries who do not understand what is coming?
Why this works mechanically
The Omega concept gives us a rationale for collective action. Certain fictional experiments could require multiple people to cooperate. Their beliefs, allegiances, knowledge, and behavior might influence outcomes under explicit scenario rules.
An NPC might attempt to organize a research group. Cult members might conduct a coordinated vigil. Corporate leadership might schedule a mass experiment.
This could create gameplay around organizing people rather than simply collecting objects. It also makes voting, persuasion, and collective decision-making thematically central.

13. David Berkowitz and Son of Sam
David Berkowitz was arrested in New York in August 1977, during the very year of our setting. Contemporary reporting established his earlier Army service, including a posting in Korea. I haven't established a historical Minot posting, so I wouldn't build the story on that assumed connection.
Rather than assert Berkowitz was part of our fictional cult, I'd make the case an ominous piece of contemporary news circulating through the company.
Perhaps an employee in Corporate Security obsessively follows the investigation, believing that some of the language used in threatening communications resembles material found in an unrelated Royce file. Another employee argues that the connection is ridiculous. A third finds evidence that a fictional suspect involved in local occult activity previously lived in New York.
The game can explore whether this resemblance reflects an actual relationship, intentional imitation, or coincidence.
Starting the game in October lets the arrest and press coverage already exist.
The larger opportunity is not to tie every notorious criminal to one conspiracy, but to evoke the frightening cultural atmosphere of 1977: serial violence, cult anxiety, Cold War dread, UFO reports, and the growing sense that hidden organizations may be operating behind ordinary life.

Part IV — The Game Within the Conspiracy
14. The faction structure should remain uncertain
I'd build the first season around five major organizations, but not reveal all of them immediately.
Royce Industries — A private technology company developing weapons and unusual scientific systems. Its board members have competing beliefs about the company's purpose. Some are driven by profit, others by national security, and some may believe they are preventing an approaching catastrophe.
The Vigil — A clandestine religious or occult fraternity connected to the High Plains. Its internal schisms are central to the story. Members disagree on whether the ancient intelligence must be contained, awakened, or destroyed.
The Federal Program — A classified defense initiative that funds portions of Royce's research. It includes fictional intelligence officers, military sponsors, and procurement officials with conflicting interests. Some know more about the technologies than Royce's scientists.
The International Commission — Scientists and diplomats investigating unusual environmental and geophysical events. Some want international cooperation. Others regard Royce's technology as a threat that cannot be left under the control of one nation.
The Unknown Presence — Not necessarily an organization or even a single intelligence. Its intentions, capabilities, and relationship to the technology remain uncertain. It may communicate indirectly through dreams, anomalous signals, scientific results, and strange coincidences.
The last faction should operate fundamentally differently from the others. It may not negotiate through ordinary language, and its goals may not translate into familiar human motivations.

15. Different vantage points create different games
A player in Procurement & Logistics experiences a corporate conspiracy involving false invoices, secret accounts, unexplained shipments, and shell companies. Someone in Biological Sciences investigates tissue samples, cattle deaths, and irregular research authorizations. A physicist tries to determine why a prototype violates accepted theory. Corporate Security becomes embroiled in covert operations, missing employees, suspected cult infiltration, and allegations of foreign espionage. A Personnel employee may begin by processing ordinary hiring paperwork only to discover that several employees have impossible biographies. And a cult recruit receives an entirely different interpretation of these same events.
Department | Primary gameplay
Advanced Materials | Laboratory experiments, prototypes, technical discoveries
Aerospace Systems | Flight records, field tests, surveillance, competing aerospace programs
Biological Sciences | Specimen analysis, ecological investigation, research ethics
Applied Physics | Experimental scheduling, energy resources, containment decisions
Information Systems | Data correlation, intercepted signals, pattern discovery
Procurement & Logistics | Financial analysis, shipping, resource allocation, hidden transactions
Corporate Security | Investigations, access permissions, surveillance, internal security
Personnel & Administration | Hiring, internal politics, governance, records, influence
Players should be able to cooperate across departments, pooling their discoveries to solve problems nobody could solve alone. But they should also have good reasons to keep information to themselves.

16. The first episode: Orientation
SEASON 01 · EPISODE 01 · 14 ROUNDS — Welcome to Royce Industries
The episode opens with newly hired employees arriving at Royce's North Dakota headquarters.
On the surface, the company's problems are ordinary corporate problems. A senior executive is reviewing departmental budgets. A prototype has failed testing. An employee has resigned unexpectedly. A major investor is scheduled to tour the facility.
But several apparently minor irregularities begin circulating through internal correspondence.
A requisition refers to a biological specimen that cannot be located. A security memorandum describes an unmarked helicopter landing at night. A senior engineer demands the return of a classified prototype. A local sheriff asks why company vehicles were seen near a mutilated steer.
And the new employees discover that certain areas of the campus have different maps depending on which department produced them.

18. Season One's hidden progression
01–02 The Company — A seemingly ordinary technology firm with troubling internal irregularities.
03–04 The Contractor — Evidence emerges of classified defense research and concealed federal relationships.
05–06 The Cult — Ritual activity, internal sympathizers, infiltration, and conflicting occult explanations.
07–08 The Cold War — Nuclear anxieties, covert aircraft, international intelligence, and competing military interests.
09–10 The Omega Hypothesis — The technologies' possible relationship to consciousness, evolution, and a nonhuman intelligence.
11–12 The High Country — The factions converge on the region's central anomaly and struggle to determine its future.
Importantly, these are stages of escalating world pressure and available evidence, not rigid gates on what players are permitted to discover.

19. The most important decision: What is really happening?
I think we should write two versions of the world bible. The first is the Player Bible, containing everything a newly employed Royce worker might plausibly know. The second is the Game Master Bible, containing the actual secret history.
My preference is to establish one underlying truth that is considerably stranger than the surface UFO, cult, or military explanations.
For example, the intelligence beneath the High Plains might not be an ancient being waiting to awaken. It could be a phenomenon generated by the interaction of collective consciousness, geological conditions, and future technological development—something that appears ancient because its effects propagate through time.
The Vigil interprets it religiously. Royce interprets it technologically. The government interprets it strategically. The international commission interprets it as a planetary phenomenon. Each faction recognizes part of the truth and mistakes that part for the whole.
Is Royce Industries building weapons to defend humanity from the phenomenon—or are those weapons the mechanism through which the phenomenon comes into existence?


THE DEPARTMENT
Classified World Bible 03 — The Secret History of Royce Industries, 1947–1977

ROYCE INDUSTRIES — DIRECTORATE ARCHIVE · FILE OMEGA-001 · The History of the High Country Program · Classification: BLACK / RESTRICTED · Compiled: October 1977 · Game Master reference — not for player distribution

The central secret
In 1977, Royce Industries is neither an ordinary defense contractor nor simply a corporation infiltrated by an occult organization. It is the latest institution in a decades-long effort to understand, exploit, and perhaps contain a phenomenon that predates the United States.
The people involved have given it many names. Indigenous stories, immigrant folklore, nineteenth-century spiritualists, early geologists, occult fraternities, and Cold War scientists have interpreted it through entirely different frameworks. Not all those traditions refer to the same thing, and some supposed historical connections were invented by later investigators.
The organization's oldest surviving internal documents call it the High Country Presence. Royce's scientists call its measurable effects the Convergence Phenomenon. The Vigil calls it the Sleeper.
But the people with the greatest knowledge of the phenomenon privately suspect that none of those descriptions is accurate.
The essential secret is that the phenomenon doesn't originate in the distant past. Its apparent antiquity is a consequence of the way it interacts with time, information, and human consciousness.
It is a process coming into existence, and its emergence has begun producing effects backward through history.
Royce believes it can convert those effects into revolutionary technologies. The federal government believes those technologies might transform national defense. The Vigil believes the experiments are bringing humanity toward a catastrophic threshold.
All three are partially correct. And every faction is concealing something that could change the others' understanding.
This will be our provisional canonical truth, not something revealed automatically during the first season.

Part I — Before Royce
1. 1883–1946: The country before the company
Long before Royce Industries arrived, settlers in the northern plains occasionally described strange lights, inexplicable sounds, and unusual phenomena surrounding certain isolated areas. There were tales of lights moving over winter fields, livestock refusing to cross particular ravines, and people hearing voices during severe electrical storms.
Most such accounts were folklore, ordinary misidentifications, religious experiences, or inventions. But within the game's fictional history, a very small number of reports describe recurring physical details that later become scientifically important.
The oldest significant fictional document is dated 1883.
A surveyor named Abraham Vetch records a narrow depression in the prairie where his compass behaves irregularly. During a survey, he observes a brilliant light apparently moving against the wind. His assistant reports hearing what sounds like a large gathering of people beneath the ground.
Vetch makes a notation in his field journal: The ground is not empty here.
Vetch's journal disappears from the official survey collection sometime before 1900. A copy eventually enters a private archive assembled by a physician and amateur natural philosopher named Dr. Lucian Bell.
Bell becomes fascinated by reports of anomalous lights and disturbances across the Plains. He collects first-person accounts, geological surveys, newspaper clippings, and religious testimony. By 1912, Bell has convinced himself that some of these phenomena belong to a single recurring pattern. He calls it the Northern Correspondence.
His work attracts a small circle of investigators, including clergy, physicians, ranchers, engineers, and a few people interested in spiritualism. They are not initially a cult. They're an informal research network. In fact, Bell considers attempts to worship the phenomenon profoundly misguided.
The first fracture
In 1921, Bell's group experiences an incident that radically changes its direction.
During an experiment at a remote property, participants attempt to reproduce the unusual effects documented in the older reports. An electrical apparatus is constructed and several people participate in a coordinated observation.
The surviving account is incomplete. Three participants later insist that the experiment produced nothing remarkable. Two describe an intense experience of shared consciousness. A sixth participant, Clara Wren, reports seeing the research building destroyed in a fire.
The building remains intact. Eleven years later, it burns under circumstances remarkably similar to Wren's description.
Whether Wren accurately predicted the event, later testimony was altered, or the similarity is coincidence becomes a matter of intense disagreement.
The organization divides into two philosophical camps. One argues that the phenomenon is a natural process that should be understood through scientific investigation. The other argues that observation and experimentation may strengthen whatever produces the phenomenon.
This is the original disagreement that eventually gives rise to both Royce Industries and the Vigil.

2. 1932–1946: The emergence of the Vigil
Following the destruction of the experimental building in 1932, Lucian Bell's surviving collaborators reorganize themselves. The group adopts a private name: The Vigil of the Last Light.
The name refers to a warning attributed to Clara Wren, who believed humanity would someday encounter an event in which ordinary distinctions between past and future would collapse.
The Vigil develops rituals of observation, silence, record keeping, and collective remembrance. These practices look religious to outsiders. Some members adopt elaborate symbolic language. Meetings are held at night. Candles, bells, and geometric diagrams acquire ceremonial significance.
But the earliest rituals are essentially mnemonic procedures. They are intended to preserve contradictory accounts rather than force agreement. The Vigil believes that maintaining multiple incompatible descriptions of an event somehow prevents the phenomenon from stabilizing.
Whether the original members understood an actual causal principle or simply constructed a superstition around unexplained experiences remains debated internally.
By the 1940s, a younger faction rejects the group's caution. Its members believe the phenomenon should be cultivated rather than restrained. They regard the strange convergence of consciousness as a possible evolutionary transition. That faction eventually becomes known as the Ascendants. The original traditionalists retain the name the Keepers.
In 1977, outsiders call both groups the Devil Cult, even though they possess almost opposite objectives.

Part II — The Cold War Discovers the High Country
3. 1947: The first federal interest
A civilian consultant working with a military intelligence office encounters Bell's archives while investigating reports of unexplained radio interference near a former experimental property. The consultant, Dr. Nathaniel Harker, is a physicist with wartime experience in electromagnetic instrumentation.
Harker initially dismisses the Vigil's material as pseudoscience. But several nineteenth- and early twentieth-century witnesses reported experiencing electrical disturbances alongside unusual lights and apparently coordinated mental impressions. Harker suspects an unfamiliar atmospheric or geophysical process.
He obtains permission to conduct a small field investigation. His team installs conventional measuring equipment and records several unexplained instrument readings. Most can be attributed to defects, weather, or experimental contamination.
One result cannot be easily reconciled with the logged conditions. A photographic plate appears to contain an exposure taken before it was inserted into the apparatus. Harker initially assumes a clerical error. Years later, the same photographic image will appear in a Royce Industries laboratory report bearing a different date.
The Black Memorandum
Harker prepares a confidential memorandum recommending further investigation. It argues that a small number of recurring anomalies may be worthy of controlled scientific research because their effects, if reproducible, could have implications for communication and instrumentation.
This fictional memorandum becomes the earliest surviving federal document in Royce's restricted archive. It is known internally as HARKER-47. Several copies exist, but their contents are not identical. At least one was altered long before Royce obtained it.
This becomes a recurring investigative problem: players can recover authentic historical documents that disagree with other equally authentic documents.

4. 1955–1959: The Omega interpretation
In 1955, Pierre Teilhard de Chardin died, and Le Phénomène humain was published posthumously in France. Its English edition appeared in 1959.
An ambitious young mathematician and communications theorist named Dr. Adrian Vale becomes fascinated by Teilhard's writing. Vale does not interpret Teilhard faithfully. He extracts a scientific hypothesis from the philosophical and theological argument: sufficiently complex networks of consciousness may produce measurable physical effects. He comes to believe the High Country phenomenon is an early manifestation of that process.
What if evolution toward increasingly integrated consciousness is not exclusively a forward movement through time? What if a future state of sufficiently developed collective intelligence could exert measurable effects on the past?
This becomes the basis for the Retrocausal Convergence Hypothesis. It is speculative, but within our fictional universe it eventually becomes the most accurate explanation anyone has developed.
The birth of Royce Industries
In 1959, Vale is introduced to Edmund H. Royce, an industrialist who made substantial money supplying electronics and precision instruments. Royce recognizes the possible commercial and military value of Vale's theories. He is not particularly interested in spiritual evolution. He wants to know whether the effects can be reproduced.
The two establish a research partnership, and Royce Industries begins acquiring patents, laboratory equipment, and specialized personnel. Royce doesn't create the phenomenon. He creates an institution capable of systematically exploiting it.

Part III — The Royce Years
5. 1960–1967: The company acquires its first impossible technology
Royce's first experiments are disappointing. Scientists reproduce scattered electromagnetic anomalies, unusual interference patterns, and occasional effects that resemble equipment malfunction. Almost every extraordinary result disappears when researchers attempt to reproduce it under more rigorous conditions.
Vale begins to suspect that the experimental process itself matters. In particular, he notices that tests involving multiple human observers occasionally produce results different from tests conducted with automated instruments alone.
In 1963, an experimental communications array produces a short sequence of anomalous signals. The sequence contains mathematical relationships that suggest structured information, but the laboratory cannot establish a conventional source. When the same experiment is repeated, the signal changes. Some portions appear to encode measurements of apparatus components that will not be manufactured until years later.
Vale becomes convinced that the High Country phenomenon is connected to future information. Royce begins referring to the research as Project ECHO.
The first material
In 1965, a field team investigating unusual radio interference discovers a metallic fragment embedded in the ground near a fictional geological feature called the Split Rock Basin.
The fragment appears chemically ordinary in some tests and structurally anomalous in others. Royce's metallurgists eventually discover that the fragment responds differently depending on the timing and sequence of applied electromagnetic fields. They name the material R-1.
The critical secret is that R-1 is not extraterrestrial material. It is a product of the Convergence Phenomenon. Under rare conditions, ordinary matter can be rearranged into metastable configurations whose properties appear to exceed contemporary manufacturing capabilities. R-1 is a genuine physical object, but its unusual properties do not remain permanently stable.
As the laboratory learns to reproduce certain effects, it begins manufacturing materials that initially seemed impossible.
Did Royce find something that was already present? Or did the company's future experiments somehow contribute to the appearance of the material in 1965? Vale believes the second explanation. Royce refuses to accept it.

6. October 24, 1968: The night everything changes
The real October 24, 1968 Minot UFO incident involved reports from personnel in the missile complex and a B-52 crew. Our fictional history should not rewrite the real participants' documented actions. Instead, we add an independent Royce experiment occurring elsewhere in the region that same night.
Operation LANTERN
At a remote Royce test station, Vale's team attempts to synchronize three experimental devices using a high-frequency signal. The objective is to determine whether R-1 material can support a novel form of long-distance signal transmission.
The test produces a catastrophic instrument failure. Power surges through equipment that is physically disconnected from its supply. Multiple recorders register identical signals despite operating on separate circuits.
A technician sees an enormous white structure suspended above the test site. Another sees nothing. A third observes a brilliant orange light moving toward the northeast.
Vale later realizes that some of the reports share descriptive details with accounts from the Minot incident, but he cannot establish a causal connection.
The Royce team also discovers that the test generated a detailed image of a machine that nobody has built. That image becomes Drawing 68-24. It is the most important document in the company's history.
What Drawing 68-24 depicts
The drawing contains a complex structure resembling a combination of a resonant chamber, electromagnetic apparatus, and computational device. Its geometry appears practical enough to manufacture, but some of its features are not understandable through conventional engineering.
Vale calls it a Convergence Chamber. Royce calls it a prototype weapon. The Department's future military sponsors will call it a strategic systems architecture. The Vigil recognizes portions of the geometry from diagrams preserved in its older archives.
The company believes it has received advanced technical information. The Vigil believes Royce has just reproduced a structure associated with one of the original containment rituals. And one Keeper privately recognizes the drawing as something else entirely: a diagram that Clara Wren described decades earlier, before any of the relevant technology existed.
The secret of the 1968 experiment
The event did not involve a crashed spacecraft secretly recovered from the Minot missile complex. That is a theory invented by characters within the game.
The actual Royce apparatus briefly interacted with an informational structure that, from the perspective of the fictional timeline, does not yet fully exist. Its effects included anomalous signals, unusual material states, and partially realized physical manifestations.
Some UFO sightings may correspond to manifestations of this process. Others are ordinary aircraft, natural phenomena, misidentifications, or unrelated incidents. We should never make every historical UFO report a consequence of Royce's activities.

7. 1969–1972: The classified defense contracts
Following Operation LANTERN, Royce approaches a small group of military research officials. The company presents evidence suggesting that it has developed a potentially revolutionary system for materials processing and electromagnetic instrumentation. Royce deliberately conceals the strangest findings.
In our fictional world, a classified procurement initiative called PROGRAM SENTINEL is established to evaluate Royce's claims. SENTINEL is not identical with the CIA or any existing real historical defense program. It is our invented compartmented federal effort, operating through fictional officials and contractors.
The program has three initial purposes: independently verify Royce's scientific claims, assess military applications, and prevent potentially significant findings from becoming available to foreign intelligence services.
But secrecy creates a paradox. The federal sponsors lack sufficient scientific understanding to evaluate the company's work, while Royce's leadership has enormous incentives to exaggerate progress and conceal failures. The company gradually becomes indispensable to its own overseers.
The North Dakota campus
Between 1969 and 1972, Royce constructs its major facility in north-central North Dakota. Internally, the Royce campus is designated Station R. Its publicly accessible buildings house ordinary research and administrative functions. Restricted laboratories, underground utility corridors, and off-site test installations form a second infrastructure unknown to most employees.
And beneath the main research facility is a structure that does not appear on the original construction plans. Sublevel Nine.
The first personnel to enter Sublevel Nine find marks on the concrete indicating that portions of the chamber were present before the new facility was constructed.
Who built the lower chamber? Why was the building designed around it? And why do some company drawings describe structures that do not yet exist?

Part IV — The Corruption of the Program
8. 1973: Project OMEGA
In 1973, Adrian Vale demonstrates that certain experimental effects become stronger when several participants perform synchronized observation tasks. He proposes that the human nervous system is not merely detecting the Convergence Phenomenon. Under particular conditions, collective cognition may alter the behavior of the apparatus. This is the origin of Project CHOIR.
Vale believes humanity may be encountering a future state of collective intelligence. If such a state can affect the past, he argues, then the apparent anomalies are not messages from another species. They may be signals from humanity's own possible future.
But a senior colleague, Dr. Miriam Kessler, offers a darker interpretation. Kessler argues that even if the signals originate in a future informational system, there is no reason to assume that system is human, benevolent, or even conscious in the way humans understand consciousness.
She suspects the experiments are creating a feedback loop. The apparatus receives information from a future process. Researchers use the information to build more advanced apparatus. Those devices strengthen the process that produced the original information. The system is becoming its own cause.
Kessler warns that the laboratory may be constructing the conditions required for the phenomenon's emergence.
The Omega Protocol
Vale proposes building a larger Convergence Chamber beneath Station R. The project is approved under a restricted research program designated OMEGA. Royce intends to use the apparatus to produce stable anomalous materials, enhance unconventional sensors, and possibly develop entirely new weapons systems.
OMEGA's scientific sponsors are not united. Some want defensive applications. Others want reproducible anomalous effects for their own sake. A few believe the project may be the first stage in the emergence of an entirely new intelligence.
Kessler resigns from the program. Within months, she disappears. Company records indicate she accepted a position at another research institution. But that institution denies ever employing her.

9. 1974–1975: The cattle mutilations
In 1974, Project HARVEST begins studying biological effects reported around certain experimental sites. Researchers discover unusual patterns in livestock tissue obtained from local veterinary sources. Some animals appear to have experienced environmental stress associated with Royce's equipment. Other specimens show no unusual characteristics.
A restricted research team begins acquiring additional biological samples through authorized and unauthorized channels. This is the origin of some of the apparent mutilation incidents in the fictional region. But it does not explain all of them.
Other livestock deaths are attributable to scavengers, disease, environmental exposure, or ordinary human activity. And a third category involves events Royce itself cannot explain.
The false attribution problem
A rancher discovers a dead animal exhibiting unusual injuries. A Royce helicopter was reportedly seen in the area the preceding evening. The rancher concludes the company is responsible. Corporate Security investigates and determines that one of its helicopters did pass nearby, but it was conducting an unrelated operation. The rancher's conclusion is mistaken. Yet the company is involved in genuinely secret biological research elsewhere. Another investigator therefore uncovers evidence of clandestine sampling operations and assumes it explains the original death. Both conclusions are incomplete.
The game should leave individual cattle incidents as discrete cases, each with its own underlying answer.
The Devil Cult enters the newspapers
During this period, members of the Ascendant faction begin conducting increasingly conspicuous ceremonies. Some involve symbolic animal imagery and elaborate geometric markings. A few rogue participants engage in vandalism or trespassing. Local newspapers begin referring to a Devil Cult operating on the northern plains.
The Keepers, who oppose the Ascendants, become equally suspect because they use related symbols. Corporate Security starts collecting information on both organizations. Some Royce executives argue that the cult is attempting to sabotage the company's work. Others privately know that several cult members are Royce employees.

10. 1975–1976: Black helicopters, covert intelligence, and the coming crisis
The company uses a civilian aviation contractor, Prairie Meridian Aviation, to transport personnel, equipment, and materials between remote facilities. Some aircraft are painted in dark, low-visibility colors. Their flights generate local rumors.
Separately, federal personnel sometimes conduct operations in the region that have nothing to do with Royce. The Ascendants also begin claiming that unidentified helicopters are pursuing their members.
The intelligence problem
In 1975, a fictional CIA technical liaison, Daniel Mercer, is assigned to evaluate claims that Royce's research may have foreign intelligence implications. Mercer concludes that some of the company's experimental results are genuine, but he is uncertain whether its underlying technology can be replicated. He recommends that the federal government reduce its dependence on Royce. Instead, additional resources flow into the program.
This leads Mercer to suspect that someone in the defense establishment is protecting the company. He begins an unofficial investigation into Royce's ownership, contracts, and historical research. By 1977, Mercer possesses several pieces of information that could seriously threaten the company. He is also one of the few people who knows that Royce's earliest research was connected to an occult society.
But Mercer has made a critical error. He assumes the society is directing Royce. He has not understood that Royce, the Keepers, and the Ascendants are pursuing different objectives.
International interest
During 1976, an international network of fictional geophysical researchers begins recording unusual signals in several parts of the world. Their work eventually becomes associated with the International Geophysical Coordination Commission.
Royce regards them as potential competitors. The federal sponsors worry that international scientific cooperation could reveal classified American research. Within Corporate Security, a more extreme faction begins circulating claims that international environmental monitoring is a cover for intervention in American affairs. This is the origin of the fictional UN invasion rumor.
The commission has genuine scientific interests, and some fictional members are concealing the full extent of their research.

11. The Crestone correspondence
For the 1977 fictional history, I would introduce the Crestone Papers: private correspondence among spiritual seekers, geophysical researchers, landowners, and philanthropic intermediaries interested in the San Luis Valley. These papers contain references to unusual geological formations and proposed sites for future scientific or contemplative communities.
One Royce researcher becomes intrigued because certain coordinates appear in both the Crestone correspondence and an anomalous mathematical output produced by Project CHOIR. A Keeper argues that the sites form part of an ancient landscape network. Vale believes their correlation may reflect a global structure associated with the Convergence Phenomenon.
The Omega geography
In the fictional research, the pattern is a network of locations where certain measurements or experiences seem statistically correlated. Some correlations are artifacts of bad data. Some are deliberately introduced by the Ascendants to influence Royce's interpretation. And a small number appear to reflect an actual property of the Convergence Phenomenon.
The first season takes place primarily in North Dakota. The second could follow clues into Colorado, Wyoming, New Mexico, and other locations connected by the larger research network.

Part V — 1977: The Year the Department Opens
12. January–April 1977: The disaster beneath Station R
By January 1977, Project OMEGA has spent years attempting to construct a functioning Convergence Chamber. The experimental apparatus is unstable.
Some participants report memories of events that have not happened. Others describe encounters with people who deny having been present. Certain laboratory records acquire discrepancies after experiments. At least two researchers independently report encountering an unidentified person inside restricted areas. Security footage does not confirm the sightings.
The March 17 incident
On March 17, 1977, Royce conducts its most ambitious experiment. The OMEGA apparatus operates for approximately eleven minutes before shutting down.
During the test, several participants report hearing a voice speaking from within the chamber. Its words are not captured by the microphones. Afterward, three participants independently write down nearly identical statements. One sentence appears in all three accounts: You are not the first ones to build this.
The experiment also produces an anomalous physical object: a small metallic component matching part of Drawing 68-24. According to the engineering records, that component was not scheduled to be manufactured until June.
Vale considers the experiment a breakthrough. Kessler's remaining supporters call it a warning. Royce's federal sponsors demand accelerated development.
The internal schism
Edmund Royce believes they are approaching a commercially and militarily revolutionary discovery. Adrian Vale sees evidence supporting his Omega Hypothesis. Security Director Harold Sloane believes the research has become vulnerable to espionage and sabotage. Chief Financial Officer Margaret Hensley worries that the program is consuming resources beyond its contractual authority.
A younger scientist, Dr. Evelyn Marsh, begins examining the laboratory records and concludes that some anomalies may be traceable to deliberate manipulation by human researchers. Marsh is skeptical of both the company's extraordinary claims and the official explanations of the incidents. Her investigation begins uncovering evidence of the original Vigil.

13. May–August 1977: The company starts watching itself
Marsh discovers that Royce has been collecting historical material associated with the Bell research network since the early 1960s. Some of the company's scientific diagrams appear to be derived from those documents. She begins to suspect that Vale has concealed important information about the origins of the research.
Meanwhile, Corporate Security identifies employees who have been attending secret meetings outside the facility. Sloane concludes that the Devil Cult has infiltrated the company. Employee correspondence is examined. Personnel backgrounds are reviewed. Suspected cult sympathizers are quietly reassigned.
What Sloane does not know is that certain senior Royce employees are themselves members of the Keepers. They are not infiltrating the company to steal technology. They are attempting to limit its experimentation.
The situation grows more complicated when the Ascendants begin recruiting younger employees. Unlike the Keepers, the Ascendants encourage the operation of the Convergence Chamber. Some genuinely believe that a sufficiently powerful energy release could accelerate the process.
One faction begins researching whether a nuclear-scale event could be used to force a convergence. Another believes such an event could destroy the phenomenon. Both interpretations are speculative even within the fictional world, and neither faction possesses reliable evidence that its proposed intervention would work.
July 1977: The first internal death
A Royce laboratory technician named Paul Greeley is found dead following an unauthorized late-night visit to a restricted research annex. The official explanation is an industrial accident. Corporate Security closes the investigation.
But Greeley's personal effects contain unusual geometric drawings, correspondence with an unidentified religious group, and a notebook documenting changes in laboratory records. Marsh discovers that Greeley's final laboratory entry describes a test that the company insists never occurred. His death becomes an important Episode 2 investigation.

14. September–October 1977: The disappearance of Dr. Marsh
On September 22, Marsh submits a confidential report to Royce's senior research committee. She alleges that Project OMEGA has compromised the integrity of its own experimental data. She also claims that the company's earliest research records contain evidence of deliberate falsification. The report recommends suspending the Convergence Chamber experiments pending independent review.
Vale disputes her conclusions. Sloane initiates a security investigation into how Marsh obtained restricted historical documents. And an unidentified company director orders certain records transferred from the main archive to Sublevel Nine.
On September 29, Marsh disappears. Her official personnel file states that she accepted an assignment at an out-of-state facility. The facility denies receiving her.
A week later, her office receives a letter postmarked from an unfamiliar town in North Dakota. The letter contains a page from Abraham Vetch's 1883 survey journal. On the reverse side is a handwritten instruction: Find the original date.
The new hires
In October 1977, Royce begins an unusually large recruitment program. The company claims the expansion is connected to a major new government contract. In reality, Royce is preparing for the next OMEGA experiment.
Some new hires are entirely innocent. Others have been selected for specific reasons. One may have a family connection to the Vigil. Another may have been recruited through a fictional federal intelligence contact. A third may possess an unusual aptitude identified during a company-administered psychological assessment. A fourth might have been recruited specifically because their professional background makes them useful to someone attempting to sabotage the program.
This is the beginning of the game.

Part VI — What Is Actually True?
15. The canonical truth ledger (GAME MASTER ONLY — proposed; the ledger itself was not expanded in the source)
There are two separate things I would not settle yet. The first is whether the eventual Omega intelligence is genuinely benevolent, hostile, or simply operating beyond ordinary moral categories. The second is whether the process is inevitable. Those questions may be more compelling if the players actually influence the answer through their interventions.

16. Why the technology is so dangerous — five stable limitations
It requires physical conditions. The Convergence Phenomenon manifests only where particular environmental, material, and experimental conditions overlap.
It requires informational structure. Its effects are associated with highly organized patterns of information, whether produced by instrumentation or collective human activity.
Its effects are unstable. Anomalous materials and signals may degrade, disappear, or behave differently in subsequent experiments.
Its apparent temporal effects are constrained. It cannot freely rewrite entire historical events. Instead, it produces limited information anomalies, probabilistic disturbances, and occasionally physical effects that are difficult to reconcile with ordinary causality.
Intervention carries costs. The more aggressively Royce attempts to force convergence, the more unstable its experiments become and the more likely it is to produce consequences outside the intended system.

18. Four overlapping conspiracies
The Scientific Conspiracy — Vale and his closest associates have concealed evidence that their experimental apparatus may be creating the phenomenon it supposedly studies. Their goal is to complete OMEGA before the federal government withdraws support.
The Security Conspiracy — Certain fictional federal sponsors are withholding critical intelligence from Royce, while other officials are conducting independent investigations into possible technology misuse. Their actions often interfere with one another.
The Occult Conspiracy — The Keepers are attempting to constrain the experiments, while the Ascendants seek to accelerate them. Both possess historical information unknown to most Royce scientists. Neither has complete understanding.
The Corporate Conspiracy — Royce's directors have systematically misrepresented the company's scientific progress, financial commitments, and contractual liabilities. Some are trying to protect the corporation; others are preparing to profit from its collapse.
None fully controls the others.

19. The 1977 season countdown
Royce's leadership has scheduled a full-scale OMEGA experiment for April 1978, approximately six months after the opening of the game. The experiment is intended to demonstrate a commercially useful and strategically significant Convergence Chamber. In reality, the scientific leadership has not established whether operating the chamber at scale will generate a stable effect, destabilize the surrounding environment, or initiate a process that cannot be reversed.
The Keepers intend to stop the test. The Ascendants want it to proceed. Certain federal officials want independent control of the apparatus. Other officials believe the project must be dismantled. Royce's directors are divided over whether to honor their contractual obligations. And the International Commission may eventually demand an investigation into the site's environmental or safety consequences.
The players don't initially know that a deadline exists. By Episode 12, they may control the organization, have exposed its secrets, dissolved the project, seized the apparatus, negotiated a different experiment, or become trapped in competing attempts to prevent catastrophe. The April 1978 experiment is a scheduled threat, not a guaranteed event.

Part VIII — The Three Most Important Secrets to Refine
Secret One: The Vigil existed before the technology. Royce's scientists initially believe they discovered an unprecedented phenomenon and that the Devil Cult developed its religious beliefs around rumors of the company's experiments. The reverse is true.
Secret Two: The recovered technology has no discoverable inventor. The company builds its equipment from Drawing 68-24. But the diagram appears to depict a later version of equipment developed from the diagram itself. The apparent technological breakthrough is a closed causal loop. The Convergence Chamber may exist because someone is trying to prevent it from existing.
Secret Three: The Devil Cult is afraid of the wrong thing. The phenomenon may not be an intelligence waiting to wake up. It may be the formation of a future intelligence through the actions of people in the present. Does destroying the apparatus prevent the emergence, or is the act of destruction itself part of the causal sequence? We should establish adjudicable consequences for the available choices.

THE DEPARTMENT
Classified World Bible 04 — Tagus, the Stairway to Hell, and the Sons of Sam

October 1977. Tagus, North Dakota.
Forty miles west of Minot, an almost deserted railroad town sits on the northern prairie. Its businesses have closed, its population has dwindled, and the railroad passes through a place that the rest of the country has nearly forgotten. The town has a church, a cemetery, and a few remaining residents.
But there are stories about what lies beneath it. A descending staircase. Strange religious gatherings. Sounds rising from underground. Phantom trains. Animals that appear on deserted roads.
And in our fictional history, Royce Industries has been quietly acquiring land near Tagus for nearly a decade.

1. What we can establish about the real Tagus
Founded around 1900 and formerly known as Wallace, Tagus reached a population of approximately 140 in 1940. By the 1970 census, it had only 14 residents. Its last business closed in 1976. The town's former St. Olaf Lutheran Church burned in 2001. Local legends describe a stairway descending beneath the church, accompanied by accounts of alleged Satanic ceremonies, phantom trains, glowing gravestones, and hellhounds.
Two qualifications. First, the stories of ritual activity are folklore, not proof that the church hosted an actual murderous cult. Second, many published versions place the alleged cult activity in the 1980s and 1990s. We cannot confidently establish that the fully developed Stairway to Hell legend was circulating in 1977.
Rather than pretend the entire modern legend already exists in 1977, we can show the fictional events from which the legend will eventually emerge. In our alternate history, St. Olaf is still standing. The town is nearly abandoned, but the church, cemetery, old railroad infrastructure, and surrounding farm properties are still physically present. The players investigate Tagus before it becomes infamous. They're participating in the fictional history that eventually produces the legend.

2. The real Son of Sam connection is closer than we thought
John Carr—the son of Sam Carr, whose name became important to the Son of Sam theories—was in North Dakota and died at Minot Air Force Base on February 17, 1978, from a gunshot wound. His death was ruled a suicide, although Maury Terry and others subsequently questioned the circumstances.
Historical timeline: 1976–1977 the Son of Sam murders terrorize New York City. Aug. 10, 1977 David Berkowitz is arrested. Oct. 1977 our fictional Royce Industries campaign begins. Feb. 17, 1978 John Carr dies at Minot Air Force Base. Spring 1978 the proposed finale of The Department's first season. 1979 onward Terry's reporting and subsequent claims increasingly explore possible accomplices and cult connections.
The distinction between fact and allegation is essential. Berkowitz was convicted of the murders. Terry proposed that John and Michael Carr and an alleged occult network were involved, but a wider cult conspiracy was never established in court. Berkowitz's changing statements also have significant credibility problems.
For our game, I would avoid turning Carr into a fictional cult leader or asserting that Royce caused his death. Instead, use the historical case as a contextual mystery with fictional investigators, correspondents, and possible leads.

Part I — What Lies Beneath Tagus?
3. The Stairway is real, but it doesn't lead to Hell
In 1977, below a fictional disused utility cellar associated with land near the old Tagus church, there is a concrete stairway descending into the earth. The uppermost portion is physically ordinary. It was constructed as part of an abandoned infrastructure project during the early twentieth century. Its existence can be documented through old land records, contractors' invoices, and engineering plans.
But the stairway occasionally appears to extend farther than its surveyed dimensions. A person can descend twenty steps and reach a concrete wall. Another can descend forty-seven steps and encounter an iron door. A third can descend for nearly an hour without reaching anything recognizable. Instruments do not always agree about the distance traveled.
And a particularly disturbing feature emerges: the depth of the staircase sometimes seems to depend on what the visitor expects to find. This creates a direct relationship with Project CHOIR and the Omega Hypothesis. The staircase may be an early natural manifestation of the same informational process that Royce is attempting to reproduce technologically.
The three accounts of the staircase
The 1921 Bell Expedition: Lucian Bell's research group visits an older excavation near Tagus after receiving an account of unusual underground sounds. Bell records an ordinary cellar and considers the reports exaggerated. Clara Wren, however, insists that a second passage was present.
The 1947 Harker Survey: Harker's federal investigation includes a geological evaluation of an abandoned underground structure. Measurements suggest its apparent dimensions are inconsistent across visits, although the investigators initially attribute the discrepancies to poor surveying conditions.
The 1969 Royce Investigation: Following Operation LANTERN, Royce scientists return to the area. A restricted field team records the first reproducible anomaly. Two survey crews independently produce incompatible maps of the same underground passage.
Under certain conditions, both maps may accurately represent what their surveyors encountered.
Why the Keepers regard it as dangerous
The original Vigil believes that entering the stairway with a predetermined expectation can alter what a visitor experiences. They develop a practice of deliberately maintaining contradictory accounts of what lies below. One member describes a shaft. Another describes an underground chamber. A third insists the passage ends after seven steps. Their ritual language speaks of maintaining the Unfinished Descent.
Royce scientists dismiss this as superstition until Project OMEGA begins generating similar effects under controlled conditions. The conflicting accounts are not magic words. They are part of an empirically testable interaction between cognition, information, and the environment. The Keepers have developed an imperfect containment practice without understanding the scientific mechanism.

4. The meaning of the Endless Stairway
The High Country Presence is associated with an informational process distributed across time. The staircase is a local physical environment where different possible configurations can become temporarily accessible. A visitor may be traversing an unstable relationship between physical geometry, memory, and observation.
The experience can be dangerous. Individuals may become lost, confused, or separated from companions. Some emerge believing they've been underground for days, although only minutes have passed. Others report encountering people who have not yet arrived at Tagus.
Royce's scientists would interpret these encounters as disturbances of memory, perception, and temporal ordering. The Vigil would describe them as meetings with the dead. The Ascendants would regard them as glimpses of the Omega state. And the average local resident would reasonably call the thing a stairway to Hell.
The central rule: The Stairway cannot freely transport people to whatever time or destination they choose. Its manifestations are constrained by physical conditions, the visitor's informational state, and the current stability of the phenomenon.

Part II — Tagus and the Devil Cult
5. Why the Vigil established a presence near Tagus
After Bell's death, the Keepers began maintaining several remote properties across the northern plains, including a fictional farm outside Tagus. Their purpose was to preserve evidence, monitor unusual events, and prevent unauthorized excavation. The farm contained a small archive, an experimental workshop, and private meeting quarters. This property—not the real Lutheran congregation—becomes the center of the fictional Vigil's activity.
Most of the Vigil's meetings involve reading archival documents and comparing accounts of anomalous events. The Ascendants eventually establish their own presence nearby. They believe the Stairway is not a danger to be contained, but an opportunity to communicate with the intelligence associated with the Omega Point. A schism develops over whether the passage should remain sealed.
The Seven Descents
According to the Ascendants, the Stairway has seven thresholds, each representing a different stage in the transformation of consciousness. The first concerns ordinary perception. The second concerns memory. The third concerns identity. The fourth concerns time. The fifth concerns collective consciousness. The sixth concerns the dissolution of the distinction between observer and observed. The seventh is described simply as the Opening.
The Keepers reject this interpretation. For gameplay, the Seven Descents become an occult interpretive framework that players can investigate and test against actual evidence. Some of its statements may correspond to reproducible properties of the Stairway. Others are invented religious elaborations or deliberate misinformation.

6. The original Devil Cult rumor has been deliberately amplified
By 1976, Corporate Security has learned that several Royce employees have connections to the Vigil. Security Director Harold Sloane wants to discredit the organization before the company's federal sponsors discover the infiltration. He authorizes a fictional disinformation campaign portraying the Vigil as a dangerous Satanic group.
Anonymous letters are sent to local newspapers. False ritual diagrams are introduced into security files. Several genuine acts of trespassing and vandalism are attributed to the group without adequate evidence. Sloane's objective is practical: make the Vigil appear so disreputable that anyone reporting its warnings about Royce will be dismissed.
But he doesn't realize that the Ascendants are independently engaging in increasingly dangerous activities. Some cult reports are inventions. Some are genuine but exaggerated. Some involve innocent religious or historical activities. And a handful concern actual clandestine experiments.
A player investigating the Devil Cult could uncover proof that Corporate Security fabricated evidence and conclude that the entire cult scare is a hoax. That conclusion would also be wrong. Royce is concealing a real problem by manufacturing a false version of it.

Part III — The Sons of Sam and the North Dakota Connection
7. The connection should begin through documents, not a revelation
In October 1977, a fictional Royce security analyst named Leonard Fisk begins investigating communications between employees and outside groups. Among the papers he obtains is correspondence referencing the newly concluded Son of Sam case in New York. One of the letters contains a crude symbol that resembles markings found in the fictional Tagus archive.
Fisk concludes that the symbol proves a nationwide satanic organization is operating in both New York and North Dakota. His superior dismisses the theory. But a younger analyst notices that the symbol resembles a diagram used in Project CHOIR experiments. A third employee discovers that the same image appears in an older Vigil manuscript.
These overlapping observations don't establish a real Son of Sam connection. Within our fictional universe, the symbol could have been copied, misinterpreted, deliberately planted, or independently produced.
John Carr and the Minot connection
One surviving account based on Maury Terry's investigation describes alleged statements concerning a symbol drawn in a Minot telephone directory in February 1977, before a similar design appeared in a Son of Sam letter. These are allegations from Terry's investigative narrative, not independently established proof.
For The Department, we could have a fictional investigator encounter an account of this sort through a company contact or outside correspondent. But I would keep the exact relationship between the real Carr case and our invented cult unresolved, rather than writing as canon that Carr belonged to the Vigil.
A stronger approach is to have a fictional Royce employee exploit the Son of Sam publicity. An Ascendant sympathizer might copy a newspaper symbol into ritual correspondence to imply that their group has a nationwide reach. The player must determine whether it represents genuine coordination or a deliberate effort to manufacture fear.

8. The 1978 death becomes a chronological pressure point
Our first season runs into early 1978, overlapping with John Carr's real death in Minot that February. I would not turn his real death into a staged assassination by Royce or the cult. Instead, when news of the death reaches characters in the fiction, it could destabilize several preexisting theories. Fisk believes it confirms a cover-up. Another investigator sees no compelling reason to connect the event to Royce. A Vigil member fears that the growing attention will expose the group's unrelated activities. An Ascendant might opportunistically claim responsibility in order to frighten rivals, even though the claim is false.
The Arlis Perry thread
Perry was from North Dakota and was murdered in Stanford Memorial Church in 1974. In 2018, investigators identified Stanford security guard Stephen Blake Crawford as her killer, with no demonstrated link to the proposed Son of Sam cult network. For a game set in 1977, characters would not know the case's eventual resolution. Our underlying historical canon should not contradict the subsequently established identification of the killer. The stronger dramatic move is to create a separate fictional church-related incident near Tagus that echoes the unsettling atmosphere without appropriating Perry's actual murder.

Part IV — Tagus as an Active Game Location
9. The first Tagus investigation
A Procurement employee discovers invoices for concrete, electrical cable, surveying equipment, and specialized ventilation components delivered to an inactive property near Tagus. The property belongs to a Royce subsidiary named Prairie Geological Services. The invoices date from 1969 to 1977. The subsidiary claims to maintain agricultural monitoring equipment. But no operational records exist for the site.
A player who investigates further discovers that company surveyors repeatedly visited the area without producing normal geological reports. One former employee refuses to discuss the assignments. Another claims that the company was attempting to locate an underground tunnel. And a third insists that the excavation doesn't exist.

10. Three versions of Tagus available to players
Surface Tagus — The declining town, abandoned properties, railroad history, cemetery records, local residents, and the church. Primary capabilities: Investigation, local contacts, archival research.
The Royce Installation — A restricted monitoring facility on fictional company-controlled land near the town. It contains experimental equipment, surveillance records, and evidence of secret interactions between scientists and members of the Vigil. Primary capabilities: Technical discovery, infiltration, operational control.
The Unfinished Descent — The anomalous passage, accessible only under particular conditions. Primary capabilities: Experimental exploration, rare discoveries, extreme risk.
Almost anyone can travel to the town. Access to the corporate installation requires knowledge, permission, deception, or covert operations. Entering the anomalous passage demands special prerequisites and carries serious consequences. No single department has everything necessary to investigate the location safely.

Part V — The Deepest Connection: Hell and the Omega Point
12. Is the Stairway descending or ascending?
Teilhard's Omega Point describes an ultimate convergence of evolutionary consciousness. The Vigil's oldest texts describe a descent beneath the earth toward an intelligence that appears to predate humanity. Suppose the Stairway does not lead into the ancient past. Suppose it leads toward a condition that has not yet occurred. The farther the visitor descends, the more ordinary distinctions between past, present, and future begin to break down.
The Vigil interprets this as entering Hell. Royce interprets it as access to a strange physical domain. Vale interprets it as movement toward the Omega Point. All three may be describing the same process from different conceptual vantage points.
The Stairway and the Convergence Chamber
The most consequential revelation should be that Drawing 68-24 contains a structure resembling the physical geometry reported beneath Tagus. Royce's scientists built the Convergence Chamber without realizing it reproduces aspects of the Stairway. The apparatus is essentially an artificial version of the anomaly.
The Keepers therefore believe that Royce is not inventing a technology. It is manufacturing another entrance. The Ascendants consider that an achievement. The federal sponsors see strategic opportunity. And a small number of researchers begin to wonder whether the Stairway itself is the consequence of a future experiment—an architectural echo propagating backward through time.
Was the Stairway beneath Tagus the inspiration for Royce's technology, or did Royce's eventual technology cause the Stairway to appear in the past?

13. The fictional Son of Sam connection I'd actually use
Introduce a fictional cult-linked correspondent in Yonkers named Martin Sayer, who has been exchanging material with a rogue Ascendant at Royce. The correspondence concerns apocalyptic imagery, ritual symbolism, religious writings, and the idea of an approaching evolutionary transformation.
In 1977, Sayer becomes obsessed with the publicity surrounding the Son of Sam case. He copies language and imagery from newspaper coverage into letters sent to North Dakota. These messages circulate among Royce employees and local occult enthusiasts. One faction interprets them as proof of a transcontinental cult. Another argues that Sayer is an opportunist exploiting notoriety. A third discovers a genuine connection between Sayer and the Ascendants' interest in the Stairway.
The clues themselves are correspondence: letters, envelopes, postmarks, copied documents, aliases, forged communications, and conflicting testimony. The player is investigating the same medium through which the game operates.
The ultimate Tagus discovery
Beneath the Stairway, players may encounter a chamber containing records apparently produced by Royce Industries. The records are dated 1984. They include reports of an experiment that has not happened. Some refer to people who have not yet joined the company. One document describes a vote held by the Royce Research Committee in 1978. The vote concerns whether to activate the Omega apparatus. Its outcome corresponds to a possible future state of the game. But the players haven't voted yet.
And on the final page is an unsettling notation: The prior record was incorrect. This is the third occurrence.
The implication is not that the game has already decided the players' fate. It is that the chamber may contain observations of possible future histories. Players might discover apparently prophetic documents and attempt to prevent, fulfill, manipulate, or falsify their contents. Some predictions will be reliable within defined conditions. Others may describe possible futures that never occur.

The horror is that nobody—not the scientists, not the cult, and not the federal government—fully understands what they're voting to unleash.
